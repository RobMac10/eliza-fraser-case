Great Sandy Island, early July 1836

We have been on this sand for nearly three weeks now. The longboat is useless. Six of the men took what they could carry and walked south. They said they would find Moreton Bay. We have heard nothing.

The people of this island came soon after we landed. They stood off at first, watching. When they saw we had little food left they came closer. One older man pointed at the pandanus and made the sign for eating. We gave him a brass button. He brought fish.

Eliza is still with us. Her husband the Captain is weaker every day. The wound in his shoulder has not closed. She sits beside him most of the hours and does not speak much. When the women of the island come she turns her face away. They have tried to cover her arms and shoulders with a mixture of grease and charcoal. She will not let them. She says it is filthy. They shrug and leave her in the sun.

Yesterday two of the men who stayed with us were taken away with a party of the island people. They went quietly. I do not know if they were asked or ordered. Eliza watched them go and said nothing. Later she told me, very low, that she believes we are prisoners. I asked her how she knew. She said, “They took our clothes. They make us carry wood. What else would you call it?”

I said the men who left us on the beach took more than the island people have. She did not answer.

This morning the Captain could not stand. Eliza held his head while he drank a little water the island women had brought in a shell. One of them stayed and watched. Eliza did not thank her.

I am writing this on the back of a chart page. If anyone finds it, know that we are alive, that the island people have not killed us, and that Eliza Fraser believes we are captives. I am no longer certain what I believe.

The light is going. The women are calling the children in. Eliza has not moved from her husband’s side.

C. Brown

What is agreed · from the 1836 file, and after

On the night of 21 May 1836 the brig Stirling Castle, James Fraser master, struck a coral reef well to the north of Moreton Bay. (Later writers often say Swain Reefs. That identification is disputed, and the wreck has not been found.) The survivors took to a longboat and a pinnace and headed south, hoping to reach the convict settlement at Moreton Bay. Some later accounts say Eliza, who was pregnant, gave birth in the leaking boat and that the baby lived only a short time. That claim is not in her official narrative of 6 September 1836, and it is not treated here as settled.

The longboat was beached about twenty miles south of Sandy Cape, on the island Europeans called Great Sandy Island and the Butchulla people call K’gari, around mid-June. Some of the crew walked south toward Moreton Bay. Captain Fraser and the first mate, Charles Brown, both died after the landing. A rescue party from Moreton Bay, guided by the convict John Graham, brought Eliza Fraser, the second mate John Baxter, and other survivors out in mid-August. They had been on the island and the nearby mainland for roughly six to eight weeks.

On 7 June 2023 the island’s official name was restored to K’gari.

Green on this page marks what can be agreed. Amber and other colours, used in Super Sleuths, mark what is still disputed or was added later.

Eliza Fraser

Eliza Anne Fraser · captain’s wife, survivor

A formal statement taken at Moreton Bay after the rescue.

Source. “Mrs Fraser’s Narrative. The Brig ‘Stirling Castle’,” Moreton Bay, 6 September 1836. Colonial Secretary’s papers (NSW), item 33054-002544, State Library of Queensland / FromThePage. Out of copyright.

What they said. “We were now portioned off to different masters, who employed us in carrying, Wood, water and bark, and treated us with the greatest cruelty.” At the close of the same statement: “During the whole of my detention among the Natives I was treated with the greatest cruelty…” When her husband lay dying, “I was always prevented from approaching him or rendering him any assistance.” Graham, she wrote, “spoke to the Natives for some time and after a good deal of altercation succeeded in getting me away.”

Could they really have seen it? She was there after the landing, and she was there when Graham came. She was not on the pinnace after it parted. She could not see what Butchulla people intended. The 1836 statement already speaks of detention and masters. The London book of 1838 goes much further.

Why might they say it? In 1836 she was a newly widowed woman giving a formal statement at a penal settlement, already the subject of a subscription. In 1837, in London, she stood to gain public sympathy and money, and, the Australian Dictionary of Biography records, presented herself as a penniless widow after she had remarried and had already been assisted in Sydney. Those facts do not prove the 1836 statement false. They are reasons to read each version in its moment.

Your questions

John Graham

John Graham · convict guide to the rescue party

A memorandum written to claim a reward, after the expedition.

Source. “Memorandum of the Real fact … of John Graham,” received 4 January 1837, Colonial Secretary’s papers, item 33054-002543, SLQ / FromThePage. Supported by Fyans to the Colonial Secretary, 6 September 1836 (33054-002546), and by Lieutenant Otter’s letter, 27 August 1836 (33054-002547).

What they said. He writes that he “freed Mrs. Frazier from Seven hundred Canniballs and savages” — the language of the penal colony, not a Butchulla account — but then describes how: “getting the tribes that claimed him as there friend to stand by him while he claimed her as his or the (spirit of his wife) he succeeded to take her from than frightfull place … and carried her upwards of 40 miles with the assistance of 4 blacks that came with him from the mountains.” A note on the same memorandum explains “their belief of all whites being spirits of their dead.” Fyans, who was not in the camp, added: “to him alone are we indebted for the recovery of Mrs Frazer.”

Could they really have seen it? He had lived among Aboriginal people north of Moreton Bay from 1827 to 1833 and, by his own and Otter’s account, could go into a camp unarmed. Otter confirms that Graham came back “with four natives” and that Eliza was waiting close by for a cloak. He was not a neutral observer.

Why might they say it? To obtain a remission or a reward, after “profferd Rewards and Promises from Capt Fyans.” That interest is openly on the page. It does not cancel the detail about a spirit-wife claim and Aboriginal assistance, which Otter independently places four Aboriginal men at the handover.

Your questions

Charles Otter

Lieutenant Charles Otter · 4th (King’s Own) Regiment

The official letter of the officer who led the rescue party.

Source. Letter to the Commandant, Moreton Bay, 27 August 1836, Colonial Secretary’s papers, item 33054-002547, SLQ / FromThePage.

What they said. He first met two survivors on Bribie Island who, he says, “I at first took to be natives as they were quite black and perfectly naked.” Of the later recovery: “Graham appeared with four natives and gave us the satisfying intelligence that he had succeeded, & that Mrs Fraser was waiting close at hand for a cloak to cover her, as she had been stripped by the natives from the very first as well as the rest … many of whom had been very unwilling to give her up.” Earlier in the same letter: “the natives who also brought the men in had shown them more kindness then any other tribe.” Graham, he says, “was obliged not only to go unarmed, but to strip himself perfectly naked when he went amongst them.”

Could they really have seen it? He saw the two men on Bribie, the handover on the sand, and Eliza when she came to the party. He did not see the camp from which Graham brought her. He waited at a prearranged mark in the sand. His phrase “in the hands of the natives” is his official English. It sits in the same letter as “more kindness then any other tribe.”

Why might they say it? He was reporting to his commandant, recommending Graham, and describing a “difficult and hazardous duty” the Governor later praised. He had reason to stress danger and success. He also recorded kindness and Aboriginal assistance, which he did not have to include.

Circle one:

Why it still matters

Back in Sydney, then later in England, Eliza’s story was told and retold. Newspapers and pamphlets described “savages,” captivity, and horrors. Money was raised for her. Books appeared. The island came to be known as Fraser Island. Those sensational versions helped to shape colonial ideas about Aboriginal people along the coast. They also sold. In 2023 the island’s official name was restored to K’gari — a change the Butchulla Aboriginal Corporation has described as respect, after a name taken away through disrespect. The wreck itself is straightforward. The weeks that followed, and what was later made of them, are not. That is why the file is still open.

Colour marking · agreed, disputed, later

Agreed facts

What more than one 1836 record supports, or what is not in serious dispute (the wreck date; the rescue in August; the 2023 name).

Disputed facts

Where the people who were there, or the official letters, do not agree (how she was held; how the captain died; who was willing to let her go).

Later additions

What appears in 1837–38 publications, later books, or modern interpretation, but not in the official Moreton Bay statements (cannibal horrors; some popular reef names; grease and charcoal as a reading).

Reliability labels

Every source in the pack is labelled high, medium, low, or unknown — for what that particular source can actually show, not as a verdict on a person.

LabelWhat it means, and an example from this file
HighA record close to the event, by someone who could have seen the thing described. Example: Otter on the handover he waited for on the sand; Fyans forwarding the statements; the official restoration of the name K’gari in 2023.
MediumA record by someone who was there, or close to those who were, but with a clear interest, a language barrier, or a partial view. Example: Eliza’s 6 September 1836 narrative; Graham’s memorandum; Otter on the camp he did not enter.
LowA later, sensational, or second-hand telling, especially where it adds what the 1836 file does not say. Example: Curtis, 1838; the 1837 New York pamphlet; newspaper “puffs” for the London appeal.
UnknownA detail that cannot yet be checked against a named record, or that consultation has not yet cleared. Example: some later reconstructions of who walked south; any Butchulla wording not yet approved for the classroom.

Reliability of Eliza’s 1836 statement: medium (she was there; she had almost no shared language; the statement already uses the English of detention). Reliability of the later London and New York versions: low to medium, and lower where they add what the 1836 report does not say. Graham: medium (he had lived in the country and went into the camp; he was asking not to be “still detained”; his dates disagree with themselves). Otter: medium to high for what he saw himself (the men on Bribie; the handover on the sand; Eliza’s condition); lower for the camp he did not enter.

Other people in the 1836 file

John Baxter, second mate — voluntary statement, 6 September 1836 (33054-002548), mainly the wreck, the parting of the boats, and the conduct of Robert Darge, Robert Dayman and Harry Youlden (spellings vary). The official statement as filed ends when those men leave the beached boat.

Joseph Corralis (also Corallas, Corrales, Carrallis), “a Native of South America” and the steward, is noted by Fyans as having given “a full corroboration” of Baxter.

Robert Darge. Fyans wrote that Darge showed “an apparent unwillingness … to give any information regarding Capt. Frazer or his lady.” A long “narrative” attributed to Darge appears later in Curtis (1838). That is a published London book, not the official 1836 file.

What’s true about the opener · the letter from Charles Brown

The letter is written to look authentic. It is not a historical document. No letter from Charles Brown on Great Sandy Island is known. He was real: the first mate (Eliza’s narrative; Baxter’s “Chief mate”; later scholarship). The page is not labelled invented, because the book’s standing rule is that what’s true sits only here.

Dramatic irony, not for the student page. Eliza’s official narrative says that after Captain Fraser’s death she and Brown were put across to the mainland in a canoe; Brown, already terribly injured (she describes burns with firebrands, and that “the flesh had fallen from his feet”), died three or four days after the crossing. She says she was prevented from taking him food. The letter’s line “know that we are alive, that the island people have not killed us” is written in a voice that does not know that. Students should meet the letter first. Teachers may return to Brown’s death after the witnesses, if they judge the class ready; it needs a content note.

Constructed scenes and details that were kept, because they carry the approved voice and are not clearly contradicted, but are not in the 1836 statements: the brass button and the pandanus sign (Eliza says fish were obtained in exchange for “articles of wearing apparel”); the grease and charcoal on Eliza’s arms (a later interpretive reading of practical care, not a detail in the official file); the shell of water; “two of the men who stayed with us were taken away” as a single witnessed scene; Brown’s spoken uncertainty on the sand; the “back of a chart page.”

The shoulder wound. The letter says “The wound in his shoulder has not closed.” It does not say he arrived on the island already wounded. Eliza attributes the wound to a spear, “a little below the blade bone,” after they were already in a camp and he had been unable, through debility, to carry a large log of wood. “Of this wound he never recovered,” and he died eight or nine days afterwards. In an early-July letter, a wound that “has not closed” can fit a spearing earlier in the stay. Teachers should not let students assume it was a wreck or boat injury.

Two wording changes only in the approved text. Everything else of the approved letter was kept.

  1. The date line. “Late July 1836” became “early July 1836.” Eliza’s narrative of 6 September 1836 says they beached about twenty miles south of Sandy Cape “about three weeks since our departure from the wreck” (the wreck was the night of 21 May, so around mid-June). Barbara Dawson, In the Eye of the Beholder (ANU Press, 2014), gives about 12 June. The letter also says “We have been on this sand for nearly three weeks now.” Early July keeps that sentence consistent with a mid-June landing.
  2. First contact. “The people of this island came on the third day” became “The people of this island came soon after we landed.” Eliza’s official narrative: “Directly we landed the Natives came down in crowds,” and at first the sight of firearms, she says, prevented violence; they procured fish in exchange for clothing. “They stood off at first, watching” was kept, as a constructed detail of cautious approach, not as a delay of two days.

Other details flagged, not changed. “Six of the men… walked south.” Eliza does not give a number: she says the seamen left, leaving the captain, herself and the two mates “totally defenceless.” Baxter names Robert Darge, Robert Dayman and Harry Youlden (spellings vary) as taking the captain’s arms and leaving, and at that point he still has Joseph Corralis and “another man who is since drowned” with the officers. Dawson (2014) reconstructs six men walking south; three reached Moreton Bay. The number six is a later reconstruction, not a figure in the 1836 statements. It was kept, with this flag, because it is the scholarly number and is not clearly false.

Uncertainties · still open

The baby in the boat. In the ADB and some later histories; not in the 6 September 1836 official narrative. Leave unsettled.

Bracewell and Russell. Still to be checked: locate the primary text of David Bracewell’s 1842 claim and Henry Stuart Russell’s version before citing them. Official 1836–37 records name Graham, not Bracewell.

Where exactly the ship struck. A reef north of Moreton Bay, 21 May 1836, is agreed. Swain Reefs is a common later identification, disputed by Geraghty (2021) in favour of Frederick Reef. The wreck has not been found.

Dates of the recovery. Graham’s memorandum disagrees with itself. Otter’s letter is dated 27 August. The ADB gives the party’s return to Brisbane as 21 August 1836.

Names and spellings. Corralis / Corallas / Corrales / Carrallis; Youlden / Youldrow / Gouldron; Dayman / Daymens; Darge / Dargo / Large. This draft uses one form on the student page and records the variants here.

The 1837 London wording. A sentence often repeated, that the Lord Mayor had “never heard anything so truly dreadful,” is cited by Iain McCalman from contemporary papers; verify against the newspaper before quoting it in the finished case. It is not quoted here.

Butchulla wording in the finished case. Quote only page-exact, cleared extracts. The Three Laws are cited from the Corporation’s website and marked for consultation.

Afterlives of the story. Modern Butchulla and scholarly readings (Foley, Behrendt, and others) argue that the captivity-and-cannibal story fed a colonial picture of Aboriginal people and helped to justify dispossession, including the name “Fraser Island.” That is an interpretation about afterlives, not a finding the 1836 file can settle on its own.

Cultural safety · reminder

Consultation first, with the Butchulla Aboriginal Corporation and those it names. AIATSIS Code of Ethics; guidelines for ethical publishing; free, prior and informed consent; agreement on what may be quoted. No sacred or secret material. Use K’gari for the island, and “Fraser Island” only when discussing the colonial renaming and its undoing. Use Butchulla as the Corporation does (sources also spell Badtjala, Badjala, Butchalla). Do not collapse Butchulla with neighbouring groups. Period words such as “savages” and “cannibals” may appear only as short, clearly attributed quotations, with a content note. They are not the book’s language. Avoid sensationalism, including the sexual-fear tropes of the captivity genre; if later sources raise them, they belong in the teacher note, not in the opener. Do not invent a Butchulla speaker or likeness. Teacher preview required.
